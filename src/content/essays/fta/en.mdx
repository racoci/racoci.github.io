# Constructive Approximate Fundamental Theorem of Algebra over Rational Complex Numbers

## Context

The standard Fundamental Theorem of Algebra relies heavily on the complete topological properties of the real numbers and the complex plane, utilizing concepts like compactness, continuous homotopies, and the Extreme Value Theorem. However, establishing the existence of roots can be problematic in strictly constructive mathematics, as the exact roots of a polynomial with rational coefficients may be irrational, meaning they do not physically exist within the field of rationals.

## Objective

The objective of this document is to prove an approximate version of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra strictly within the rational complex field. We will construct a sequence of points—using only rational arithmetic, finite combinatorial counting, and algebraic inequalities—that is proven to be a Cauchy sequence, and whose polynomial evaluation converges to exactly zero in algebraic quadrature. We will achieve this without ever assuming the existence of real numbers, square roots, continuous angles, or traditional Euclidean metric completeness.

**Definition 1.1: The Rational Complex Field**
Let the ordered field structure be defined as:
$$
\mathbb{Q}(\textcolor{#3b82f6}{i}) = \{\textcolor{#fbbf24}{a} + b\textcolor{#3b82f6}{i} \mid \textcolor{#fbbf24}{a}, \textcolor{#fbbf24}{b} \in \mathbb{Q}\}

**Definition 1.2: The Polynomial**
Let the complete rational complex polynomial be defined as:
$$
\textcolor{#10b981}{P}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}) = \textcolor{#3b82f6}{c_d} \textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}^{\textcolor{#3b82f6}{d}} + c_{\textcolor{#3b82f6}{d}-1 \textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}^{\textcolor{#3b82f6}{d}-1} + \dots + \textcolor{#f43f5e}{c_0}}
$$
A polynomial of degree:
$$
\textcolor{#3b82f6}{d} \ge 1
$$
With rational coefficients:
$$
\textcolor{#f43f5e}{c_k} \in \mathbb{Q}(\textcolor{#3b82f6}{i})
$$

**Definition 1.3: Algebraic Quadrature**
To avoid irrational square roots, we define the quadrature function:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{a}+b\textcolor{#3b82f6}{i}) = \textcolor{#fbbf24}{a}^2 + \textcolor{#fbbf24}{b}^2
$$

**Definition 1.4: Algebraic Inner Product**
We define the real algebraic projection inner product as:
$$
\langle \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1}, \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2} \rangle = \text{Re}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1} \cdot \overline{\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2}}) = \textcolor{#fbbf24}{a}_1\textcolor{#fbbf24}{a_2} + \textcolor{#fbbf24}{b}_1\textcolor{#fbbf24}{b_2}
$$
Where we have the complex vectors:
$$
\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1} = \textcolor{#fbbf24}{a_1} + \textcolor{#fbbf24}{b_1} \textcolor{#3b82f6}{i}
$$
$$
\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2} = \textcolor{#fbbf24}{a_2} + \textcolor{#fbbf24}{b_2} \textcolor{#3b82f6}{i}
$$
And the quadratic norm relation:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w}) = \langle \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w}, \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w} \rangle
$$

**Definition 1.5: Discrete Quadrants**

## II. Fundamental Rational Theorems

Because we cannot use the standard Euclidean triangle inequality in metric spaces, we must establish strict algebraic equivalents using quadrature.

**Theorem 2.1: The Rational Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality**
For any rational complex vectors, the following inequality holds:
$$
\langle \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1}, \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2} \rangle^2 \le \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1})\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2})
$$
*Proof:*
By direct algebraic expansion in the field:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1})\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2}) - \langle \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1}, \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2} \rangle^2 = (\textcolor{#fbbf24}{a_1}^2 + \textcolor{#fbbf24}{b_1}^2)(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{a_2}^2 + \textcolor{#fbbf24}{b_2}^2) - (\textcolor{#fbbf24}{a}_1\textcolor{#fbbf24}{a_2} + \textcolor{#fbbf24}{b}_1\textcolor{#fbbf24}{b_2})^2
$$
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1})\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2}) - \langle \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1}, \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2} \rangle^2 = (\textcolor{#fbbf24}{a}_1\textcolor{#fbbf24}{b_2} - \textcolor{#fbbf24}{b}_1\textcolor{#fbbf24}{a_2})^2
$$
Because the square of any rational number is strictly non-negative:
$$
(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{a}_1\textcolor{#fbbf24}{b_2} - \textcolor{#fbbf24}{b}_1\textcolor{#fbbf24}{a_2})^2 \ge 0
$$
Therefore, the inequality holds. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 2.2: Rational Quadrature Sum Bound**
For any finite set of $m$ elements, the quadrature of the sum satisfies:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}\left(\sum_{k=1}^m \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_k}\right) \le m \sum_{k=1}^m \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_k})
$$
*Proof:*
First, note that for any two elements of the sum:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{u} - \textcolor{#fbbf24}{v}) = \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{u}) + \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{v}) - 2\langle \textcolor{#fbbf24}{u}, \textcolor{#fbbf24}{v} \rangle
$$
Because we have:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{u} - \textcolor{#fbbf24}{v}) \ge 0
$$
We obtain the limit:
$$
2\langle \textcolor{#fbbf24}{u}, \textcolor{#fbbf24}{v} \rangle \le \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{u}) + \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{v})
$$
Expanding the quadrature of the sum of $m$ terms:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}\left(\sum_{j=1}^m \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_j}\right) = \sum_{j=1}^m \sum_{k=1}^m \langle \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_j}, \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_k} \rangle \le \sum_{j=1}^m \sum_{k=1}^m \frac{1}{2}(\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_j}) + \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_k}))
$$
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}\left(\sum_{j=1}^m \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_j}\right) \le m \sum_{k=1}^m \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_k}) \ \blacksquare
$$

**Corollary 2.3: Algebraic Lower Bound (Triangle Alternative)**
If the error quadrature is strictly less than one-ninth of the base:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2}) \le \frac{1}{9} \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1})
$$
Then the quadrature of the sum satisfies:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1} + \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2}) \ge \frac{1}{3} \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1})
$$
*Proof:*
By direct algebraic expansion:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1} + \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2}) = \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1}) + \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2}) + 2\langle \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1}, \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2} \rangle
$$
Applying Cauchy-Schwarz:
$$
\langle \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1}, \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2} \rangle^2 \le \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1})\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2}) \le \frac{1}{9} \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1})^2
$$
Because quadratic ordering is valid in any ordered field, we obtain:
$$
\langle \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1}, \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2} \rangle \ge -\frac{1}{3} \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1})
$$
Substituting into the expansion:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1} + \textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_2}) \ge \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1}) + 0 - \frac{2}{3} \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1}) = \frac{1}{3} \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{w_1}) \ \blacksquare
$$

## III. Polynomial Bounding and Alignment

We decompose the polynomial into a leading term:
$$
\textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}) = \textcolor{#3b82f6}{c_d} \textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}^{\textcolor{#3b82f6}{d}}
$$
And an error term:
$$
\textcolor{#f43f5e}{E}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}) = \sum_{k=0^{\textcolor{#3b82f6}{d}-1} \textcolor{#f43f5e}{c_k} \textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}^k}
$$

**Theorem 3.1: Dominance of the Leading Term**
There exists a rational square region with a bounding radius such that for any boundary point, the leading term dominates the error, ensuring the complete polynomial never touches the origin.
*Proof:*
We define a rational domain radius explicitly as:
$$
\textcolor{#fbbf24}{R}^2 = 1 + 9\textcolor{#3b82f6}{d}^2 \sum_{k=0}^{\textcolor{#3b82f6}{d}-1} \frac{\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#f43f5e}{c_k})}{\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#3b82f6}{c_d})}
$$
For any boundary point, we have:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}) \ge \textcolor{#fbbf24}{R}^2 > 1
$$
Which gives the inequality:
$$
\textcolor{#3b82f6}{d} \cdot \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#f43f5e}{c_k}) \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z})^k < \frac{1}{9\textcolor{#3b82f6}{d}} \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#3b82f6}{c_d}) \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z})^{\textcolor{#3b82f6}{d}}
$$
Applying the quadrature sum limit (Theorem 2.2) to the error:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#f43f5e}{E}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z})) \le \textcolor{#3b82f6}{d} \sum_{k=0}^{\textcolor{#3b82f6}{d}-1} \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#f43f5e}{c_k} \textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}^k) < \frac{1}{9} \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}))
$$
By Corollary 2.3, the total polynomial quadrature is bounded below:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#10b981}{P}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z})) = \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}) + \textcolor{#f43f5e}{E}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z})) \ge \frac{1}{3} \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z})) \ge \frac{1}{3} \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#3b82f6}{c_d}) \textcolor{#fbbf24}{R}^{2\textcolor{#3b82f6}{d}}
$$
Since this limit is strictly positive, the polynomial never touches the origin on the boundary. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 3.2: Algebraic Alignment**
For any point on the boundary, the terms reside in the same algebraic half-plane, meaning the position inner product is positive:
$$
\langle \textcolor{#10b981}{P}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}), \textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}) \rangle > 0
$$
*Proof:*
Expanding the real inner product:
$$
\langle \textcolor{#10b981}{P}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}), \textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}) \rangle = \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z})) + \langle \textcolor{#f43f5e}{E}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}), \textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}) \rangle
$$
Since we have:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#f43f5e}{E}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z})) \le \frac{1}{9} \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}))
$$
By Cauchy-Schwarz, the error projection is bounded by:
$$
\langle \textcolor{#f43f5e}{E}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}), \textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}) \rangle \ge -\frac{1}{3} \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}))
$$
Thus, the alignment is strictly guaranteed:
$$
\langle \textcolor{#10b981}{P}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}), \textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}) \rangle \ge \frac{2}{3} \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z})) > 0 \ \blacksquare
$$

## IV. Combinatorial Topology

**Definition 4.1: Rational Lipschitz Mesh**
By rational region bounding, we calculate the strict rational Lipschitz constant $K$:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#10b981}{P}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{u}) - \textcolor{#10b981}{P}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{v})) \le K \cdot \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{u} - \textcolor{#fbbf24}{v})
$$
We discretize the boundary into a mesh with a rational step size so as to guarantee:
$$
K \cdot \delta^2 < \mu
$$

**Definition 4.2: Discrete Winding Number**
The discrete quadrant winding number is computed by the unrolled counter variation divided by the cyclic order:
$$
\textcolor{#fbbf24}{\Delta} = \frac{\textcolor{#fbbf24}{U}(k) - \textcolor{#fbbf24}{U}(0)}{4}
$$

**Theorem 4.3: Additivity of Winding Numbers**
For the product of two complex sequences, the total rotation is additive:
$$
\textcolor{#fbbf24}{\Delta}(A \cdot B) = \textcolor{#fbbf24}{\Delta}(A) + \textcolor{#fbbf24}{\Delta}(B)
$$
*Proof:*
The combinatorial quadrant transitions add up with localized corrections:
$$
\textcolor{#fbbf24}{U}_{AB(j)} = \textcolor{#fbbf24}{U}_A(j) + \textcolor{#fbbf24}{U}_B(j) + \epsilon(j)
$$
As the loop closes perfectly on the mesh, the corrections match at the end, resulting in additivity. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 4.5: Discrete Rouché's Theorem**
The winding number of the complete polynomial matches the degree of the leading term:
$$
\textcolor{#fbbf24}{\Delta_P} = \textcolor{#fbbf24}{\Delta_L} = \textcolor{#3b82f6}{d}
$$
*Proof:*
Since the curves never occupy opposite quadrants due to orthogonal inner product alignment, the unrolled counters divergence is strictly bounded:
$$
|{\textcolor{#fbbf24}{U_P}(j) - \textcolor{#fbbf24}{U_L}(j)}| \le 1 \quad \forall j
$$
Any rotation mismatch would require the difference in counters to pass continuously through the value $2$ (opposite quadrants), which would violate the orthogonal alignment. Thus, the counts match. $\blacksquare$

### 4.7 The Three Geometric Invariants of Rouché

To prove geometrically and directly that the complete polynomial $\textcolor{#10b981}{P}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z})$ and the leading term $\textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}) = \textcolor{#fbbf24}{a}_n \textcolor{#fbbf24}{z}^n$ topologically align, we analyze their vector relationships and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality as the domain radius $\textcolor{#fbbf24}{R}$ expands.

#### 4.7.1 The Orthogonal Projection Invariant
The mathematical condition for $\textcolor{#10b981}{P}(t)$ and $\textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(t)$ to share the same winding number is that the angle between them remains strictly acute ($< 90^\circ$). This is guaranteed if the inner product $\langle \textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(t), \textcolor{#10b981}{P}(t) \rangle$ is strictly positive, forcing $\textcolor{#10b981}{P}(t)$ to lie entirely inside the positive orthogonal half-plane of $\textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(t)$.

#### 4.7.2 The Geometric Error Disk Constraint

#### 4.7.3 Asymptotic Scaling and Radius Convergence
As the complex plane radius $\textcolor{#fbbf24}{R}$ grows, the norm of the dominant term $\|\textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}(t)\|$ grows at a higher-order polynomial rate ($\textcolor{#fbbf24}{R}^n$), while the error disk radius $\|\textcolor{#f43f5e}{E}(t)\|$ grows at a lower-order rate ($\textcolor{#fbbf24}{R}^{n-1}$). This asymptotically forces the entire error disk away from the origin $(0,0)$, locking the vector $\textcolor{#10b981}{P}(t)$ within the positive half-plane and aligning its winding number with the leading term.

## V. The Quadtree Bisection Algorithm

We iteratively construct our Cauchy sequence starting with $Q_0$ (where $\textcolor{#fbbf24}{\Delta}(Q_0) = \textcolor{#3b82f6}{d} \neq 0$).

At each step $n$:

1. We bisect $Q_n$ at its rational midpoints into 4 smaller sub-squares: $Q_{n,1}$ (Top-Right), $Q_{n,2}$ (Top-Left), $Q_{n,3}$ (Bottom-Left), $Q_{n,4}$ (Bottom-Right).
2. By combinatorial conservation, the internal mesh transitions evaluate in opposite directions and cancel out. Thus, $\sum_{\textcolor{#3b82f6}{i}=1}^4 \textcolor{#fbbf24}{\Delta}(Q_{n,\textcolor{#3b82f6}{i}}) = \textcolor{#fbbf24}{\Delta}(Q_n)$.
3. Because $\textcolor{#fbbf24}{\Delta}(Q_n) \neq 0$, at least one sub-square must possess a non-zero winding number.

**Axiom 5.1: Deterministic Selection Rule**
A polynomial of degree $\textcolor{#3b82f6}{d}$ may possess multiple roots, meaning multiple sub-squares could simultaneously exhibit non-zero winding numbers. To construct a singular, unambiguous sequence targeting exactly one pseudo-root, we define $Q_{n+1}$ as the **first** sub-square in the canonical ordered list $(Q_{n,1}, Q_{n,2}, Q_{n,3}, Q_{n,4})$ that satisfies $\textcolor{#fbbf24}{\Delta}(Q_{n,\textcolor{#3b82f6}{i}}) \neq 0$.

Let $\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z_n} \in F$ be the geometric center of the uniquely chosen square $Q_n$.

## VI. Proof of Convergence

**Theorem 6.1: The Sequence is Cauchy**
The sequence $(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z_n})$ generated by the bisection algorithm is a Cauchy sequence.
*Proof:*
The side length of $Q_n$ is strictly $\textcolor{#3b82f6}{L}_n = \frac{2\textcolor{#fbbf24}{R}}{2^n} \in \mathbb{Q}$. For any indices $m > n$, both $\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z_n}$ and $\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z_m}$ reside inside $Q_n$. The maximum squared distance between any two points in $Q_n$ is strictly bounded by the squared diagonal:

$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z_n} - \textcolor{#fbbf24}{z_m}) \le \frac{8\textcolor{#fbbf24}{R}^2}{4^n}
$$

For any rational tolerance $\epsilon > 0$, there exists an integer $n$ large enough such that:
$$
\frac{8\textcolor{#fbbf24}{R}^2}{4^n} < \epsilon
$$
Thus, $(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z_n})$ is strictly a Cauchy sequence by definition. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 6.2: Quadrature Convergence (The Approximate FTA)**
The polynomial evaluation of the sequence converges to zero:
$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} \textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#10b981}{P}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z_n})) = 0
$$
*Proof:*
Assume, for contradiction, that there exists a rational constant $\lambda > 0$ such that the image of $Q_n$ never falls below:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#10b981}{P}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z})) \ge \lambda
$$
Because $\textcolor{#10b981}{P}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{z})$ is bounded by the rational Lipschitz constant $K$ on $Q_n$, the maximum image variation inside $Q_n$ is strictly bounded:
$$
\textcolor{#a855f7}{N}(\textcolor{#10b981}{P}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{u}) - \textcolor{#10b981}{P}(\textcolor{#fbbf24}{v})) \le K \cdot \frac{8\textcolor{#fbbf24}{R}^2}{4^n} \quad \forall \textcolor{#fbbf24}{u}, \textcolor{#fbbf24}{v} \in Q_n
$$
For sufficiently large $n$, this internal variation becomes strictly less than $\lambda$.
If the entire perimeter of $Q_n$ maps to points farther than $\lambda$ from the origin, yet the maximum variation across that perimeter is strictly smaller than $\lambda$, the mapped boundary is confined to a tight region that algebraically cannot enclose the origin.
Uniquely, Axiom 5.1 structurally guarantees that $\textcolor{#fbbf24}{\Delta}(Q_n) \neq 0$ for every $n$. This is a direct contradiction.
Therefore, the assumption must be false. The sequence's quadrature inevitably drops below any rational bound $\epsilon > 0$, resulting in convergence to exactly zero. $\blacksquare$

This completes the constructive proof that a Cauchy sequence honing in on a pseudo-root can be rigorously established entirely within the rational complex field $\mathbb{Q}(\textcolor{#3b82f6}{i})$.
